The AG website, built step by step

This is the filmed build of the AG website — our home page, composed in AGrrr! from a blank profile. Every step below carries two things: a plain-words walkthrough written for somebody who has never built a website, and the session's own technical caption under the shot, kept for the record.

If you have never opened AGrrr! at all, read the step-by-step first-website guide first — it teaches the clicks this page assumes. And you do not have to build anything this ambitious to ship a good site: every record and every panel move you see here is the same handful of gestures the guide walks.

The build session, steps 01 to 10

Step 01 — Open the app, and give the site its pages

In plain words: open AGrrr!, press Start building on the welcome card, and the canvas is yours. The first page is called Home and lives at your site's root. More pages are one press of + Add page in the page bar — here the session adds Products, Blog and Contact, and the site's menu derives itself from those pages the moment they exist. You never draw a menu by hand.

Step 02 — Bring in the art, and dress the whole site once

In plain words: before building, the session imports the site's own pictures and fonts in the Assets tab (Choose file…, Import font), then opens the Theme tab and sets the site's colours and type once — the tokens every page inherits. This is the dress-it-once move: one panel edit reaches every page you will ever make, and switching the whole site between dark and light is one swap.

Step 03 — The page's skeleton: one big glass panel

In plain words: the page's backbone is a single library record — a frosted glass panel that composes itself as the whole page. In the Library, search for the block, drag it onto the canvas, and fill its parameters: parameters are just gaps in a finished block that your answers fill — how wide, how blurred, how rounded. This record then offers named regions (hero, title, menu, main, footer), which work as labelled drop zones: every block that follows lands inside the band you aim at.

Step 04 — The hero's picture, as its own layer

In plain words: the hero band gets its art from a second record — the hero art overlay. You tell it which image, how wide, and where to pin itself; the picture rides as its own layer, so it can run past the panel's bottom edge without being cropped. Nothing gets squeezed into a box that cuts it off.

Step 05 — The title, and the round click ball

In plain words: the title band takes two small records. The title box holds one word — you type it, and pick the site's title font. The click ball is a linked element: it answers a real click with its animation. Both carry their small-screen behaviour on the record — above a chosen width the disc sits on the hero's edge, below it the disc centres over the title's own text. You decide each behaviour once; the app keeps it.

Step 06 — The menu, which builds itself

In plain words: the gutter takes three records — a menu search, the page tree, and a theme toggle. The page tree is the clever one: it is derived from the pages you created in step 01, so renaming or adding a page updates the menu by itself. A fourth record, the mobile menu bubble, adds the hamburger and drawer that carry the same menu on narrow screens — the app knows which one to show at which width because the record declares it.

Step 07 — The words, in the main band

In plain words: the page's text goes into the main band, record by record: prose blocks for the paragraphs, and feature rows whose media slots take a picture and a caption — you pick the asset, type the caption, and the row dresses itself in the site's theme. Links are typed per record; the ones pointing at pages that do not exist yet stay deliberately empty.

Step 08 — The footer, one record

In plain words: the footer is a single record on the footer band: the copyright line, a cookies link, and contact slots. Empty slots are fine — the record tolerates them — so the session ships the footer without inventing an address.

Step 09 — Read the page back, in real units

In plain words: with the compose done, the session checks its work: the geometry table beside the canvas prints every placement in vh and vw — the same screen units the built CSS carries — and the ball answers a real click on the canvas. What you measure here is what the site ships.

Step 10 — Measure against a real screen

In plain words: the View menu picks a tracked screen size, and the canvas grows measuring tapes and a red-edged overlay for that viewport; the selected block reports exactly where it sits, in the same units the browser will use. It is a ruler built into the app — the instrument this session refined the page against.

From export to a live IONOS site

Step 11 — Export the whole site

In plain words: open the File menu, press Export…, and export as a site: every page is written, plus the files a real host expects — .htaccess, robots.txt, a 404 page, a handover README. The app never guesses your public address; you type it, and a site with no address still exports.

Step 12 — Give it your host's details

In plain words: the Deploy tab takes your hosting connection once: protocol (FTP or SFTP — prefer SFTP when your host offers it), host, port, username, remote path, and the password. The password goes into Windows' own keychain, never into the project. The fields below carry stand-in values, and the walkthrough spells out what an IONOS customer fills in their place — no real host, user or secret appears anywhere on this page:

  • Host: ftp.your-domain.example — the FTP host your own IONOS hosting panel names for your contract.
  • Port: 21 over FTP, 22 over SFTP — whichever of the two your panel's connection details list.
  • User: your-ftp-username — the FTP user IONOS gives you for the hosting space.
  • Password: your-ftp-password — the matching password, kept in the panel only for the deploy.
  • Remote path: /annoyinggoods — the folder the domain binds to, the way IONOS binds each domain to a folder under the FTP account; in absolute terms /home/www/annoyinggoods.

Step 13 — Test the connection first

In plain words: press Test connection before the first upload — it answers whether the host, the port and the credentials line up, and it is the same test you would run against any host.

Step 14 — Deploy, and read the receipt

In plain words: press Deploy to … and the site uploads; the report names every file it wrote. The site now on your host is the same bytes the export delivered.

Step 15 — Open it live

In plain words: the deployed home is opened in a real browser, served the way your host serves it. This is the moment the whole session was for: your page, live at your domain, built without writing a line of code by hand.

Step 16 — Check it at five widths

In plain words: a site is visited by phones as well as desks, so the deployed home is re-filmed at five widths — 1700, 1450, 1200, 950 and 700 pixels — and every small-screen decision made earlier is checked live: the menu moves into its drawer, the ball centres over the title, the rows stack for the phone.

Your turn

The same handful of gestures

Nothing in this session is out of your reach: every step is a press, a drag, or a field filled in. If you have not built before, the first-website guide teaches those gestures on a small page first, and the download puts the app on your machine. What you saw take sixteen steps here took the guide's fifteen too — they are the same road at different pace.
