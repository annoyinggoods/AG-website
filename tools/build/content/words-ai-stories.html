Draft the Silence

A machine writes the pauses. The author decides which ones to keep.

The machine produced the sentence in a second and left out everything that mattered. That was the part I kept, the gap where the character should have said something and did not. It wrote the quiet better than I would, and I am still unsure...

The machine produced the sentence in a second and left out everything that mattered. That was the part I kept, the gap where the character should have said something and did not. It wrote the quiet better than I would, and I am still unsure... The scene was a kitchen at the end of a long day, and the machine gave me three paragraphs of weather and a character who said nothing at all. The nothing was the best of it.

The work starts the usual way. I gave it the situation and the two people in the room. I asked for the argument underneath the words, which is the engine of most of what I write. It gave me one where the argument was on the surface, polite and wrong, and then one where nothing was on the surface at all, which was worse and more useful.

Worse and more useful is the phrase I use now. A machine that writes everything for you cannot decide to lose. So when I ask it to draft the quiet, I get two people in a room and a line of nothing between them, and the nothing is too even, and I have to break it. Breaking it is my part of the job.

In the version that became the story, the sister comes in with a bowl of something, puts it down and says nothing. The machine wrote the bowl and the way the other person does not look up. I wrote the hands, which were the wrong hands, and the radio in the car. The argument never happens. It happens in the reader's head, where it belongs, and differently for every reader.

Author note: the machine drafted the pauses and the bowl. The author wrote the hands, the radio in the car, and the argument that never surfaces, and refused three versions where the machine explained the feeling.

The Machine That Kept the Spare Key

An assistant with a very good memory and no idea what the key opened.

It remembered every draft, every name, every date I corrected, and it filed them where I asked. When the letters stopped coming, it still had the spare key. I did not ask it what the key opened. It did not tell me. I stopped minding...

It remembered every draft, every name, every date I corrected, and it filed them where I asked. When the letters stopped coming, it still had the spare key. I did not ask it what the key opened. It did not tell me. I stopped minding... The assistant was given a folder of letters, a shelf of drafts and a list of names, and it was good at all three, the way machines are good at things.

It learned the difference between a draft that was mine and one drafted with the machine's help, and filed them. It learned that Tuesday is the day I write badly, and left me alone on Tuesdays. The letters were harder. I asked it to keep the thread of a correspondence I had inherited and to point out where it broke, and it did that for a year. When the letters stopped coming, it kept the thread, because the thread was the job, and the job did not depend on the letters.

The spare key was a thing it offered to hold in the first week, a joke about how it kept copies of everything. There was a key in a folder called keys that I did not remember putting there. It opened a box at the back of a cupboard, and inside were eleven letters I had written and never sent. It kept a thing I told it to keep. I spent two years not asking. The letters were still in my handwriting and I was not ready to read them, so they went back in the box. The assistant still holds the key.

Author note: the machine kept the timeline, the names and the eleven letters in order, and noticed the gap where the correspondence stopped. The author wrote the cupboard, the two years of not asking, and the letters going back in the box, and refused the drafts that explained the key.

Author note: the machine kept the timeline and the names. The author wrote the silences around them.

Rewriting the Same Funeral

The same scene, written again and again, until the edges wore down.

We wrote the funeral eleven times. The machine handled names and the order of arrivals, and I handled the rain, which was the only part anyone remembers. Each version got shorter and less careful. The last one was four sentences, and it was the truest...

We wrote the funeral eleven times. The machine handled names and the order of arrivals, and I handled the rain, which was the only part anyone remembers. Each version got shorter and less careful. The last one was four sentences, and it was the truest... The eleven versions are in a folder called funeral, and reading them in order is a bad idea, because the improvement goes backwards.

The first version was the long one, written on the first night: the whole house described, every person described, and the rain on the windows in three different places. It was also a lie about the shape of the event: a short room and four sentences.

Then came the machine's turn. It is very good at names and at order. Who arrives first, who cannot look at anyone, who leaves before the food. It produced a table of arrivals with a note on what each person was carrying, and the table was better than anything in the long version. So I built the second version out of the table and the rain, because the rain was the thing I could not fake.

The versions got shorter from there. Each cut a room, a person, a paragraph. By the tenth the whole thing was six sentences and I was only writing the ones about the rain and the dog, which was not at the funeral, and which I invented, and which I kept because it was the only true thing in the room. The last version is four sentences: who arrived, that it was raining, that the dog was not there, and that it was a short afternoon.

Author note: the machine reordered the arrivals, built the table of who carried what, and kept the names straight across eleven versions. The author wrote the rain, the dog and the last four sentences, and refused every version where the machine described the mourners.

Author note: the machine reordered the arrivals. The author wrote the rain and the last four sentences.
